Asymptotic properification of rainbow-C4 colorings
Date: 2026-10-15
Status: elementary consequence of the triangle-removal lemma; literature novelty
is not asserted. The argument below has been independently checked, not Lean checked.

Definitions. A B-coloring is an edge coloring of a simple graph in which every
four-cycle has four different colors. A proper B-coloring is also proper at
vertices. Write M(n,q) and P(n,q) for the maximum number of edges in such graphs
on n vertices with at most q colors, without and with properness respectively.

THEOREM. There is a function r(n)=o(n^2), independent of q, such that every
B-colored n-vertex graph can be made properly B-colored by deleting at most
2r(n) edges. In particular,
                 P(n,q) <= M(n,q) <= P(n,q)+o(n^2),
where the error is uniform over all palette sizes q.

Proof.
1. If uv and vw are distinct edges of the same color, u and w have exactly one
   common neighbor, v. Indeed another common neighbor x would give the
   non-rainbow four-cycle u-v-w-x-u. All four vertices are distinct.

2. Greedily choose an inclusion-maximal family F of edge-disjoint monochromatic
   paths of length two. Let t=|F|. The two legs of different chosen paths are
   disjoint as graph edges. Their endpoint pairs are also distinct: two paths
   with the same endpoint pair would have the same center by step 1 and thus
   be the identical path.

3. Take three disjoint copies A,B,C of the original vertex set. Orient the
   endpoints of each chosen path arbitrarily, writing it u-v-w, and add the
   three edges u_A v_B, v_B w_C, and u_A w_C. The resulting tripartite graph T
   has exactly t edge-disjoint triangles.

   Edge-disjointness: the A-B and B-C legs cannot repeat because the original
   chosen paths are edge-disjoint; the A-C shortcut cannot repeat because
   endpoint pairs are distinct. Edges in different bipartite pairs of parts
   cannot coincide.

   No extra triangle: every triangle in T has an A-C edge u_A w_C belonging to
   some chosen path u-v-w. Its B vertex x_B gives original edges ux and xw.
   The unique-common-neighbor conclusion of step 1 forces x=v. Thus this is
   the selected triangle associated with that shortcut.

4. The triangle-removal lemma implies that a graph on 3n vertices consisting
   of edge-disjoint triangles and no other triangles has t=o(n^2) triangles.
   More explicitly, if t>=epsilon n^2 along a sequence, every triangle-free
   deletion would require at least t edges (one per edge-disjoint triangle).
   The removal lemma would then force Omega_epsilon(n^3) triangles in T, but
   T has exactly t<=n(n-1)/4 triangles, a contradiction for large n.

5. Delete both original legs of every path in F. If the remaining coloring
   were improper, it would contain a monochromatic two-edge path disjoint
   from all selected paths, contradicting maximality. Deletion preserves B.
   This costs 2t=o(n^2) edges. The construction and its bound do not mention q.

Quantitative formulation. Let R(N) be the largest number of edge-disjoint
triangles in an N-vertex graph having no other triangles. Then one can take
r(n)=R(3n); the classical (6,3)/triangle-removal theorem says R(N)=o(N^2).
The displayed upper bound t<=n(n-1)/4 uses two original edges per path.
Primary reference for the triangle-removal lemma: Jacob Fox, A new proof of
the graph removal lemma, Annals of Mathematics 174 (2011), 561-579:
https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2011/174-1/p17
This reference supplies the established removal theorem, not an attribution
of the properification corollary to Fox.

Consequences and limitations.
- A proof of P(n,n)=o(n^2) is equivalent to the original M(n,n)=o(n^2).
- Any asymptotic constant upper bound for proper B-colorings transfers with
  the SAME constant to arbitrary B-colorings. The earlier elementary
  minimum-degree/matching selection gave only quadratic loss in density.
- This is not a proof that either extremal function is o(n^2).
- It does not make the palette a perfect matching factorization. In a
  perfect B-coloring every vertex sees every color. Properification merely
  ensures that a vertex sees any given color at most once.

Ordinary near-regularization.
After deleting low-degree vertices from a dense proper B graph, split each
remaining vertex into a bounded number of vertices, partitioning its incident
edges into groups with sizes between delta/2 and delta. This preserves edges,
colors, and B: a four-cycle in the split graph maps to a four-cycle in the
original graph, since its two distinct incident edges at a clone arise from
different original edges and opposite clone-identifications would require
one original edge to have been duplicated. This makes degrees comparable,
but leaves missing colors at each vertex.

Related published tool: Kim, Lee, Liu and Tran, Rainbow Cycles in Properly
Edge-Colored Graphs, Combinatorica 44 (2024), Lemma 3.1, uses this vertex
splitting to obtain delta/2 <= degree <= delta with at most 4e/delta vertices.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00493-024-00101-7
This citation is for the splitting operation, not for our properification
claim or any perfect-B completion theorem.

Perfect-B completion status. No density-preserving completion theorem has
been established here. Extracting an ordinary regular factor does not solve
the palette problem: its coloring can use more colors than its degree.
Selecting vertices that see every color of a chosen large subpalette asks
for a complete bipartite subgraph in the vertex-color incidence graph, which
is not supplied at linear scale by ordinary density alone. These observations
identify gaps in naive reductions, not an impossibility theorem for all
possible regularization or completion constructions.
